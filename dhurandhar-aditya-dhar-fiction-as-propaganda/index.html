How Aditya Dhar Sells Stories: Dhurandhar and the Fiction Loophole

Fiction gives permission. Dhurandhar never claims to be real, and that is exactly why its politics travel so easily.

By Sai Charan, 8 Dec 2025, 4 min read

Dhurandhar runs on a simple premise: fiction gives permission. Once a film says it is not real, the viewer stops resisting the ideology inside the narrative. Aditya Dhar builds the entire film around that psychological loophole. It never claims to be based on real events. It does not need to. It creates a world so familiar that the audience recognises it instinctively, and that recognition feels like truth.

Dhurandhar is a turning point in Dhar’s filmmaking, not because he changes his ideology, but because he finds a more sophisticated way to protect it. He moves from representing real incidents, as he did in URI, to fictionalising reality in a way that resembles the Padmavat model. The shift is strategic. It opens a loophole that makes the propaganda harder to challenge and easier to spread.

From URI to Dhurandhar

URI was built on actual events. The surgical strikes were a documented military operation, and Dhar constructed the film around them with patriotic exaggeration but without denying the real-world anchor. Accuracy was secondary. What mattered was the credibility that real events automatically grant. The audience walked in assuming the film was based on something factual, which let the slogans and emotional beats travel as national truth.

The line “ghar mein ghus kar maarenge” was used not only to project patriotism but also to target Indian Muslims, painting them as internal enemies linked to the film’s imagined Pakistan. The line between fictional villain and real citizen collapsed, because Dhar had already coded patriotism as hostility.

Dhurandhar uses a completely different tactic. Dhar has realised that fiction is a stronger shield than fact. By altering names, tweaking timelines and building a synthetic national crisis that resembles real political conflicts without directly depicting them, he gains total freedom. He can imply real events without claiming them. He can provoke political emotion without inviting scrutiny. He can deliver the same message URI carried, now protected by the sentence he tweeted before release: the film is fictional and not based on any real incident. That disclaimer is the entire architecture of the strategy.

The Padmavat template

The approach mirrors the Padmavat phenomenon. Padmavat adapted Jayasi’s poem, which is entirely fictional, yet presented it with such visual seriousness that audiences accepted it as history. Alauddin Khilji, as the film imagined him, became a real figure in public memory even though that version of him never existed. The danger did not come from the plot. It came from the confidence with which people treated the film as evidence. Anyone who corrected the history risked confrontation, because the film had already overwritten their understanding of the past.

Dhurandhar follows that template with greater calculation. The world feels indistinguishable from geopolitical reality. The antagonists resemble familiar political enemies. The rhetoric mimics actual nationalist narratives. The stakes mirror current polarisation. But every detail is adjusted just enough to keep plausible deniability. It is a fictional story that behaves like real history. This is how propaganda travels without being named.

A feeling, not an argument

Dhar’s filmography has consistently served an intoxicating blend of right-wing messaging, anti-Pakistan sentiment and high-voltage nationalism that encourages the audience to cheer for aggression as a moral duty. Dhurandhar adds a new layer. It converts political messaging into an emotional reflex rather than an argument. The fictional veneer makes the audience less defensive.

They do not feel they are being indoctrinated. They believe they are simply being entertained. In that relaxed state, the film delivers its worldview straight into emotional memory. It becomes harder to challenge because it was never presented as a thesis. It was experienced as a feeling, and anyone who corrects a historical claim becomes the villain the film has already warned the audience about.

The certificate of authenticity

A crucial part of Dhar’s credibility in the public imagination is his identity as a Kashmiri Pandit. That identity is used in marketing, interviews and audience perception as a certificate of authenticity on Kashmir-related narratives. His perspective is treated as truth rather than one of many political experiences. It gives his films added weight, a belief that he speaks from lived history even when the films deliberately fictionalise incidents for strategic ambiguity. The combination lets him promote a narrow political stance while appearing emotionally neutral.

Camouflage

Dhurandhar enters a political climate far more polarised, impatient and emotionally volatile than the Padmavat era. Today’s audiences treat emotionally charged cinema as an extension of national discourse. The climate rewards majoritarian narratives, distrusts fact-checking and treats emotional experience as truth. When a film like this blurs fiction and reality, the audience does not separate them. They carry its simplified binaries into their perception of actual citizens and actual tensions.

That is why it has political impact even while denying political intent. It gives viewers a ready-made emotional script for interpreting India’s conflicts: a world where aggression is duty, suspicion is patriotism and dissent is betrayal. The film may insist it is fiction. The audience will carry its messages into the real world with the confidence of having witnessed something true.

Dhurandhar is not dangerous because it lies. It is dangerous because it lies persuasively.

Cinema in such an atmosphere is not passive. It shapes the common sense of a nation. It tells people who belongs, who threatens, who must be feared and who must be silenced. It may deny its politics, but its impact will be political. It is not a political film pretending to be entertainment. It is an ideological project using entertainment as camouflage, and that camouflage is more refined than in URI, because fiction makes the message harder to criticise and easier to believe.

That is the evolution of Aditya Dhar’s method. And that is the real impact.

Sai Charan
