The Angry Man Formula: Misogyny and Toxic Masculinity in Pan-Indian Cinema

Baahubali, Pushpa, KGF, Animal, Salaar: the pan-Indian hero is angry, armed and dominant. The women exist to watch him.

By Sai Charan, 10 Jan 2024, 2 min read

Baahubali, Liger, Pushpa, RRR, KGF, Kantara: these pan-Indian blockbusters share one thing. An angry hero with a strong physique, intense expressions and moments of aggression.

Whether it is Pushpa Raj in Pushpa or Ranvijay Singh in Animal, being an angry, dominant male is becoming a defining quality of the pan-Indian hero. Nearly every pan-Indian poster shows its hero branding a weapon. KGF has a hammer, Pushpa has an axe, Salaar has a gun. These heroes indulge in chest-thumping, flaunt hyper-masculine physiques, play to the male gaze and, of course, feature in an item song. Most troublingly, they often touch or control women against their will, all in the name of heroism. On the flip side, men who do not conform to these standards are portrayed as weak or funny.

A revolution with a blind spot

In the past decade the film industry has seen a revolution, with pan-Indian films drawing crowds across regional and language barriers. Despite that groundbreaking shift, misogyny in films continues. Female characters are designed only to be looked at. They are reduced to objects, with little screen time, existing only to push the hero’s story forward or validate his masculinity.

Here is where it gets problematic. These heroes receive immense love and worship from audiences, and that raises a basic question: have we, as a society, normalised this behaviour? These excessively physical films are more popular than ever.

Anger without a reason

In earlier films, the hero’s anger was usually justified by social injustice or personal tragedy. Now pan-Indian films are setting a new standard, where heroes are unapologetically mean and angry without any meaningful cause.

No matter how forward the industry looks at the box office, the revolution has not touched the deeper issues.

Misogyny and toxic masculinity have become the success formula for pan-Indian films. Which makes me wonder: are these films simply designed to appeal to men? In a way, the story always centres on a man, is written by a man, and is meant primarily for a male audience.

Sai Charan
